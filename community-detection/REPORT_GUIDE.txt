=====================================================================
REPORT GUIDE
Community detection: Louvain, Leiden, Infomap, Bayesian SBM, Spectral
=====================================================================

This is how the report is structured and how each person writes their
part, so the four of us produce one consistent report instead of four
mini-reports.

Target length: about 4 pages of main text (it can run slightly over).
Extra figures go in the appendix.



*********************************************************************
BEFORE YOU START: WHAT ARE "SEEDS" AND HOW MANY RUNS ARE THERE?
*********************************************************************

A "seed" is just a number that fixes the randomness, so a random
process gives the same result every time you rerun it. We use seeds
in TWO different places. Don't mix them up.

  1. GRAPH SEEDS: 2 per setting  (the "s1" and "s2" in folder names)
     We BUILT 2 different random versions of each easy/medium/hard LFR
     graph, e.g. mix_mu0.4_s1 and mix_mu0.4_s2. Same difficulty,
     different random network. This is already done. You don't
     touch it.

  2. ALGORITHM SEEDS: 5 per graph  (the "seed" column, 0 to 4, in CSVs)
     Most algorithms are a bit random inside (which node they look at
     first, where K-means starts, etc.). So the harness runs YOUR
     algorithm 5 times on EVERY graph with seeds 0, 1, 2, 3, 4.
     This happens automatically when you type  python run.py <algo> all

So for ONE algorithm, the harness does:

  Experiment 1 (LFR)
    6 mixing graphs  (mu 0.2 / 0.4 / 0.6, each with s1 and s2)
  + 2 scale graphs   (10k and 50k nodes)
  = 8 graphs x 5 algorithm seeds          = 40 runs

  Experiment 2 (email)
    1 graph x 5 algorithm seeds            =  5 runs

  (plus the parameter sweep and ablation runs, also automatic)

YOU DO NOT AVERAGE ANYTHING BY HAND.
make_figures.py averages all the runs for you:
  - In the figures, each point = the average of all runs, and the
    small error bar = how much the runs disagreed.
  - In results/summary_table.csv, each row = one graph + one
    algorithm, already averaged over the 5 seeds (NMI = mean,
    NMI_sd = spread).
  - For the mu = 0.2 / 0.4 / 0.6 points in Fig 1, the s1 and s2 graphs
    are averaged together too (2 graphs x 5 seeds = 10 runs per point).

In the report you write ONE number per result, like:
  "NMI = 0.93 +/- 0.02 (mean +/- sd over 5 seeds)"

If a run on the 50k graph times out, the harness skips the other seeds
for that graph. That is expected, and it counts as a result.
*********************************************************************

---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE STORYLINE (everyone should know this)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

  "We test 5 community detection methods from 4 different paradigms on
   synthetic graphs where we know the answer, find exactly where and
   why each one breaks, then check whether those lessons survive on a
   real network."

Every section should serve that sentence. If a paragraph doesn't help
answer "which method, when does it break, and why", cut it.

Why these methods (use this in the introduction). Each one has a
different idea of what a community is:

  Part 1  Louvain, Leiden
          Paradigm: modularity optimisation
          Community = denser inside than a random graph would predict

  Part 2  Infomap
          Paradigm: information flow
          Community = where a random walker gets trapped

  Part 3  Bayesian SBM
          Paradigm: statistical inference
          Community = groups that explain the edge pattern, in the most
          compressed way

  Part 4  Spectral clustering
          Paradigm: linear algebra + K-means
          Community = nodes that sit close together in the graph's
          eigenvector space

2. OVERALL STRUCTURE
---------------------------------------------------------------------

The report goes: both experiments with all algorithms, then a deep dive
into each algorithm, then one consolidated comparison.

  1. Introduction                        1/3 page    Owner: Editor
     The problem, why it's hard (no labels in practice), our question,
     why these 4 paradigms, contributions in 3 bullet points.

  2. Experimental setup                  1/2 page    Owner: Editor
     Datasets (LFR + email-Eu-core), metrics (NMI, ARI, modularity),
     5 seeds per graph, 15-min time limit, hardware, and "all methods
     run through one shared harness".

  3. Experiment 1: LFR benchmark,        3/4 page    Owner: ___________
     all algorithms
     Figures: Fig 1 (accuracy vs mu), Fig 2 (runtime vs n),
              Fig 3 (stability)
     WHAT happened, stated with numbers. Who stays accurate as mu rises,
     who breaks first and at what mu, who is fastest, who times out.
     Keep it factual. The WHY belongs in section 5.

  4. Experiment 2: email-Eu-core,        1/2 page    Owner: ___________
     all algorithms
     Figures: Fig 6 (email placed on the LFR curve),
              Fig 10 (which departments get merged or split)
     WHAT happened. Scores on the real network, where email sits on the
     LFR difficulty curve (its own mu), and whether the LFR ranking held.

  5. Algorithm analysis, one per part    about 1/2 page each
     5.1 Louvain + Leiden                Owner: ___________
     5.2 Infomap                         Owner: ___________
     5.3 Bayesian SBM                    Owner: ___________
     5.4 Spectral clustering             Owner: ___________
     Each owner explains how their method works, then WHY it performed
     the way it did in Experiments 1 and 2, plus its own parameter and
     ablation tests. Template in section 3 of this guide.
     Each part has its own figure: figures/part1_louvain_leiden.png,
     part2_infomap.png, part3_sbm.png, part4_spectral.png

  6. Consolidated comparison and         1/2 page    Owner: Editor
     recommendation
     Figures: Fig 4 (modularity gap), Fig 8 (disconnected communities),
              results/summary_table.csv
     WHICH method to use when. One ranking table across all criteria,
     the cross-method insights, the recommendation, and limitations.
     Template in section 4 of this guide.

  Appendix                               any length  Owner: anyone
     Fig 5a/5b (all sweeps together), Fig 7 (all ablations), Fig 9,
     full summary table, failures.csv.

Total: about 4 1/2 pages.

THE THREE-LAYER RULE (this stops us repeating ourselves):
  Sections 3-4 say WHAT happened.       ("Infomap drops to 0.38 at mu = 0.6.")
  Section 5 says WHY, per algorithm.    ("Because at high mixing the random
                                          walker no longer gets trapped...")
  Section 6 says WHICH to choose.       ("For weakly separated communities,
                                          prefer ...")
If you catch yourself explaining WHY in section 3, move it to section 5.

3. ALGORITHM ANALYSIS: TEMPLATE FOR EACH PART (section 5)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Every part uses the same headings in the same order, so the four
sections line up. About 350 words each, plus your part figure.

  5.X <Algorithm name> (<Author, Year>)

  How it works (max 80 words)
    Its idea of a community in one sentence, then 3-4 numbered steps.
    Mechanism only, no history.

  Settings we used (max 30 words)
    What can be tuned, what we used and why.

  Performance in Experiments 1 and 2 (max 100 words)
    Point back to Figs 1, 2, 3 and 6 for YOUR method. Explain WHY it
    did well or badly using the mechanism above. Include one clear
    success and one clear failure.

  Parameter sensitivity (max 50 words)
    Your sweep (panel b of your part figure). Where is the best value?
    Is it the same on LFR and email? What if you don't know it?
    (SBM has no parameter: say why that's a strength and a weakness.)

  Ablation (max 50 words)
    Your on/off tests (panel c). What does each component contribute?

  Strengths and weaknesses (max 40 words)
    2 of each, each tied to the mechanism, not generic ("fast",
    "popular").

PART 1 (LOUVAIN + LEIDEN) gets about 450 words, since it covers two
methods. Do the full template for Louvain. Then add "Leiden: what it
changes" (max 100 words): the refinement step, the Louvain flaw it
fixes (Fig 8), and what that did to the results. The pair is our
cleanest ablation, since the two differ in exactly one component.

PART-SPECIFIC POINTS TO COVER:
  Louvain + Leiden  Resolution limit (lost small communities at 50k,
                    Fig 9); refinement vs disconnected communities.
  Infomap           Collapse at high mu; how much restarts (trials_10)
                    help; Markov time behaviour.
  Bayesian SBM      Chooses K itself; degree correction; runtime cost
                    and timeouts.
  Spectral          Was given the true K (always say so); what happens
                    with wrong K or eigengap-estimated K; eigenvector
                    cost at scale.

Also add one row per method to the comparison table (Table 1, goes in
section 2). Columns:
  Method | Objective | Search strategy | Must be told K? |
  Tunable parameter | Randomness from | Complexity

4. EXPERIMENT AND CONSOLIDATED SECTIONS: HOW TO WRITE THEM
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Sections 3, 4 and 6 use a four-step pattern for each finding.

  CLAIM      One sentence, the finding.
             e.g. "Leiden stays accurate longest as mixing increases."

  EVIDENCE   Point to the figure and give numbers.
             e.g. "At mu = 0.6, Leiden keeps NMI = X vs Y for Infomap
             (Fig. 1)."

  MECHANISM  In sections 3-4, keep this to a pointer: "(see 5.2)".
             In section 6, give the cross-method reason.

  SO WHAT    What a practitioner should take away.

BANNED: sentences that only restate the figure ("As shown in Fig. 1,
NMI decreases as mu increases.") without a point.

Section 6 must include this ranking table:

  Criterion                       | Louvain | Leiden | Infomap | SBM | Spectral
  Accuracy, easy (mu = 0.2)       |         |        |         |     |
  Accuracy, hard (mu = 0.6)       |         |        |         |     |
  Real network (email)            |         |        |         |     |
  Speed at 50k                    |         |        |         |     |
  Stability across seeds          |         |        |         |     |
  Needs K / tuning?               |         |        |         |     |

Then answer:
  - Does any method find a partition with HIGHER modularity than the
    truth (Fig 4)? What does that say about modularity as an objective?
  - Did LFR predict the email results? Why or why not?
  - Recommendation: "use X by default, Y when..., avoid Z when...".
  - Limitations: one real dataset, 5 seeds, Colab hardware, Spectral
    given the true K.


How this covers the marking criteria:

  Produce results ourselves
    -> All numbers come from our harness runs (results/raw/)
  Strengths and weaknesses
    -> Section 5 (each part), section 6 ranking
  Parameter settings ("e.g. K in K-means")
    -> Section 5 parameter sensitivity; Spectral's K sweep is the
       direct equivalent
  Ablation of key components
    -> Section 5 ablation, where every method has at least one test
  Key factors affecting performance
    -> Section 3 (mixing, size), section 5 (why), Fig 3 (seeds)
  Compare methods
    -> Sections 3, 4 and 6
  Success and failure cases
    -> Section 5 (one each per part), Fig 10; timeouts as results
  Other analysis
    -> Modularity gap (Fig 4), LFR-to-real bridge (Fig 6)


---------------------------------------------------------------------
5. STYLE RULES (so it reads as one voice)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

  - Numbers: 2 decimals for NMI and ARI, reported as mean +/- sd over
    seeds (e.g. 0.93 +/- 0.02). Runtime in seconds with 2 significant
    figures. A timeout is written as "> 900 s", not left blank.
  - Names: always "Louvain", "Leiden", "Infomap", "Bayesian SBM" and
    "Spectral clustering". Write the Greek letter mu (μ), not "mu".
  - Figures: only use the ones from make_figures.py, so colours and
    markers are the same everywhere. Every figure is referenced in the
    text, and every caption says what to notice.
  - Fairness: always mention that Spectral was given the true K when
    you compare its accuracy with the others.
  - Tense: present tense for findings ("Leiden recovers..."), past tense
    for what we did ("We ran...").
  - Voice: "we", short sentences, no filler ("It is worth noting
    that...").
  - Citations: Blondel et al. 2008 (Louvain), Traag et al. 2019
    (Leiden), Rosvall & Bergstrom 2008 (Infomap), Peixoto 2014
    (Bayesian SBM), Ng, Jordan & Weiss 2002 (Spectral), Lancichinetti
    et al. 2008 (LFR) and SNAP for email-Eu-core.
